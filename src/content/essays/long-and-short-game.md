---
title: The Long Game and the Short Game in Sales
tags: [Sales, Business Development]
order: 1
---

Golf has a saying that every salesperson feel that they can tattoo it on their heart: "Drive for show, putt for dough." A nice and far tee shot gets applause, but nobody's ever won a tournamen off the driving range alone. The best score comes down to what happens beyond the fairway near the hole. Selling works the same way - and knowing which game you're playing, at which moment, is often the difference between a deal that closes and one that quietly dies in a pipeline report after all the glamorous noise has settled.

**The long game is prospecting, positioning, and relationship-building.** It's the ardulous work of understanding an account's business, showing up consistently before there's anything to sell, and earning the right to be in the room when a decision finally gets made, the at-bat moments! Like a well-placed drive, its value isn't obvious in the moment - a good long game doesn't close deals, but it really sets up the shot that will. This is where you invest in industry knowledge, build ample social wealth through multiple relationships inside an account rather than betting on one champion, and resist the urge to pitch before you've earned the trust to be heard. Enterprise and complex B2B deals live or die on the long game, because the sales cycle is long enough that whoever has built the deepest relationship and the clearest understanding of the buyer's world usually wins - regardless of who has the flashier demo, smoother POC, or even gets the first phase of the contract.

**The short game is what happens once you're near the green: negotiation, objection handling, and the final touch to close.** This is precision work, not power. It's reading the specific terrain in front of you - the exact concern a legal team has, the internal politician who needs a win, the budget cycle that's about to close - and playing the right shot for that situation, not a generic one. Short-game players who try to "drive" their way through a stalled negotiation, pushing harder instead of reading the situation, usually blow the deal past the hole entirely.

**Knowing which game to play comes down to reading where you are in the cycle.** Early on, when there's no active opportunity yet, long game is the only game - trying to force a close before there's a real need just burns the trust bridge you'll want later. Once a deal is validated, active and moving, the long game keeps working quietly in the background (deepening relationships, multi-threading the account) while the short game takes over on the specific mechanics of getting to yes: pricing conversations, procurement, the final redline on a contract. The best closers don't abandon the long game once negotiation starts; they just layer the short game dedicately on top of it.

The founders and sellers who struggle most tend to over-invest in one game at the expense of the other. All long game and no short game produces beautifully warm relationships that never turn into revenue. All short game and no long game produces a string of aggressive, transactional closes with no recurring or upselling business or referrals behind them. The pros play both balancedly, patient and relationship-driven from a distance, precise and determined up close.
